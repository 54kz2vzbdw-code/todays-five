## The eight

### 1. The Watch talks to the server itself, through `SupabaseTransport`

**The call.** The Watch derives keys, opens the envelope, pulls, merges and pushes through the same `TodaysFiveCore` the phone and the CLI use; the phone is never on the data path.

**Who made it.** Instructed. Phase 3 prompt §2: *"The Watch talks to the server itself through the core's `SupabaseTransport` over whatever network watchOS provides. Pull on foreground and on every wrist raise that shows the app; push on every change… The Watch never asks the phone for data; it only takes the links."* `WatchInfo.plist` sets `[REDACTED] = true` to match.

**What it bought.** A wrist that works with the phone in another room or off — the one scenario that justifies a Watch app at all rather than a mirror. The Watch has no rules of its own: `WatchStore.swift` is 776 lines and every document rule in it is a call into `Model.*`, which is why the rollover-agreement test (a Watch in Kiritimati and a phone in Chicago produce byte-identical documents) is a core test and not a Watch one. The App Intent runs on the wrist with no phone. And the doorbell fix in Phase 4 landed in `SyncEngine.push()` once and reached the Watch, `tfive` and the intent together — that is only possible because all three are real clients of the server. The live run pushed rev 6 → 13 from the wrist and `tfive show` read every one back.

**What it costs now.** A second holder of `W`: every list's edit secret sits in the Watch's Keychain (`AfterFirstUnlock`, not synchronizable — the phone's rule). A lost Watch is a lost phone in that respect; not a new kind of exposure, but a second copy of it. A second radio client: every tap is a `put` plus a 138-byte doorbell over the wrist's own TLS session (Bluetooth-proxied through the phone when it is near, Wi-Fi or cellular when it is not), and a poll on every wrist raise. The link hand-off exists only because the Watch needs the secrets — `WatchLink.swift` 351 lines, `WatchLinkReceiver.swift` 229, `WatchLinkSender.swift` 192, `WatchLinkTests.swift` 357 — about 1,100 lines that a phone-mediated design would not have needed in that shape (it would have needed a different channel, for documents, which is not smaller).

**What it will cost later.** Nothing that grows with lists or kits. What grows is platform surface: every future server-facing feature (a realtime subscriber, presence) must be written for watchOS's suspension model — a push begun in the last moments of foreground may not complete, and the store's `dirty` flag plus the next occasion is what carries it. The round already handled the shape of that (the "occasions, never a timer" rule). A Watch on a train with no phone: works, and syncs when it finds a network; that is the buy, not a cost.

**The alternatives.** (a) The Watch as a terminal for the phone — the phone owns the data, the Watch shows what the phone sends over `WatchConnectivity`. Cheaper in secrets (none on the wrist) and in radio (one client), and it is what the prompt was written to rule out. It fails exactly where a Watch is useful: `isReachable` was measured asymmetric on this pair, `transferUserInfo` did not deliver at all on the simulator, and the phone app is a `WKWebView` with no Swift copy of any list — so "the phone owns the data" means either a Swift model on the phone that does not exist or driving the page through `evaluateJavaScript` while it happens to be open. (b) A hybrid that relays through the phone when reachable to spare the wrist's radio: complexity for a battery gain nobody has measured; no.

**What changing course would cost today.** Rewrite the engine plumbing in `WatchStore` (roughly 300 of its 776 lines), delete the hand-off (about 1,100 lines with tests), add a phone-side model or bridge, lose the intent on the wrist. Every user sees it: the Watch stops working away from the phone. It could only ship as a whole build.

**Recommendation: keep.** The alternative loses the case that justifies the app, and the cost it removes (a secret on the wrist) is the same exposure the phone already carries, guarded the same way. No pushback was warranted; the prompt's reasoning was right and the round's execution proved it on a live list.

### 2. The phone hands the Watch only links, and the Watch keeps its own Keychain

**The call.** One `[REDACTED]` slot carrying `{ v, at, links: [{ id, mode, origin, nickname, name }] }`, applied only when `at` is newer than the last applied stamp; the Watch vaults what it is named and drops what it is not.

**Who made it.** Instructed. Phase 3 §2: *"the phone app sends the vault's links (secret, mode, origin, nickname) with `[REDACTED]` on first pairing and whenever the vault changes. The Watch stores them in its own Keychain. The Watch never asks the phone for data; it only takes the links."*

**What it bought.** A channel with one shape and one rule. An application context is a last-value-wins slot the system replays on launch, so the design needs no ordering, no request/response, no reachability gate (`isReachable` was measured lying). The stamp makes the doubled delivery free and out-of-order delivery safe, and "the phone is the authority on what the Watch holds" is what makes 1.12's *Remove from this device* reach the wrist with no code of its own. A vault of links is a few hundred bytes against a 256 KiB ceiling (the watch agent read `[REDACTED] = 262144` out of the runtime binary).

**The rule was bent within a round, and what that tells you.** Phase 4 put the two Secret kits' palettes on the same payload — `WatchLinkPayload.extra["kits"]`, `WatchLinkSender.setSecretKits`, 1,178 bytes with both (the suite prints it). So "only the links" was not the load-bearing rule. What held, and what the kits were made to obey, is narrower: *the slot carries state, never events; the phone is the sole authority on what the wrist holds; nothing on it is a document.* Absent-means-remove applies to the kits exactly as to the links. The Phase 3 sentence "hands over links and never data" was the slogan; the invariant underneath it survived the bending intact, which is the sign that the design was sound and the slogan was merely too short. The write-up should say the invariant rather than the slogan, because the next person to add a field will read the slogan and think they are breaking a rule.

**What it costs now.** The clock hazard the round wrote down and did not fix: the sender's stamp is `max(now, lastSentAt + 1)` — monotonic per process — and the Watch's high-water mark persists, so a phone clock that ever runs ahead poisons the channel until real time catches up. The receiver has to read both `[REDACTED]` and the callback because the simulator contradicted itself about which fires. And the Watch can never hold a list the phone has not named.

**What it will cost later.** Adding fields is free — proven twice now, since `extra` passthrough carried the kits with `v` still 1, and a future `slot`/`accent` hint would ride the same way. What is not free is the day the Watch wants to *originate* a list (paste a link on the wrist): the authority rule is one-directional, and making it two-directional means the phone's `VaultReconciler` accepting a link it did not see in its own registry — a real redesign, not a field.

**The alternatives.** Ship the document too (the phone could read the page's decrypted `tf/v3/list/*` copies through `evaluateJavaScript`): a list appears on the wrist before its first pull, at the cost of a second source of truth and up to 96 KB per list sitting in a slot that outlives the app; no. The Watch as terminal: call 1. No Keychain on the Watch (secrets in memory, re-fetched from the phone each launch): fails precisely when the phone is away.

**What changing course would cost today.** Nothing needs changing in the shape. The clock hazard is a sender sequence beside `at` (a counter, or sender-id plus sequence — the round named both): additive, about twenty lines and one test, invisible to users.

**Recommendation: keep**, with two small changes. Restate the invariant in `DECISIONS-apple.md` as "state not events, phone is the authority, never a document" now that the kits have shown the slogan was not the rule; and close the clock hazard with a sequence while it is cheap and additive rather than after the day a phone's date is set wrong.

### 3. The kit table reaches Swift as a hand-regenerated fixture, turned into Swift by `KitsGen`, held honest by a JavaScriptCore drift test

**The call.** `node test/tools/gen-kits.mjs` writes `test/fixtures/kits.json` (18 kits, 13 pairs, 42 KB); the `KitsGen` prebuild plugin turns it and `watch-fonts.json` into `Kits.generated.swift` at build time; `KitFixtureTests` evaluates live `theme.js` in a `JSContext` and fails if the fixture no longer matches.

**Who made it.** Chosen, from the two options Phase 4 §2 offered: *"generate the Swift table from `theme.js` at build time the way `ConfigGen` already generates config (note that `ConfigGen` is a prebuild command since Phase 3 and cannot use executables built from source — work within that), or write a test that fails when the two drift. Do not hand-maintain a second copy of the palette."*

**The reason it gave, checked.** The round said 62 of 314 hex tokens appear nowhere in `theme.js`'s source because they come out of `finalize() → ensure() → oklch()`. Measured here: of the 16 hex tokens × 18 kits the fixture carries (288 slots), **54 are absent from the source at HEAD, and all 18 kits have at least one** — every `hairSolid` is computed; Dark has 11 of 16 computed; Harbor 8. The kits agent reproduced the round's denominator exactly (288 slots plus 26 bare-hex `boxDoneBg`/`strikeBg`/`barBg` = 314; 62 absent at the Phase 4 base, 58 at HEAD once the accent revert made four of them literal again). Whichever way it is counted, one token in five is derived, and the derived ones are the contrast-bearing greys. A Swift plugin that extracted literals with a regex would look correct on sixteen kits and be silently wrong on exactly the tokens that hold the floors. The reason holds.

**Does the drift test run here?** Yes. `review/raw/baseline/swift-test.log` line 362: `drift: live theme.js reproduced the fixture — 21396 bytes, 18 kits, 13 pairs`. And there is a second copy nobody mentioned in the eight: `test/theme.test.js` line 501, *"the kit fixture the Swift core and the Watch read still is theme.js"*, evaluates the fixture's own `expr` against live `CURATED` in Node. So drift fails two suites, and both are step 2 of the release checklist.

**The day someone edits `theme.js` and runs neither suite.** `xcodebuild` and `swift build` succeed on the stale fixture and the Watch ships last week's palette; nothing at build time knows. That is the hole the round's own write-up says the design exists to prevent, and it is only half-closed. A person who edits a colour and follows the checklist is caught twice; a person who does not follow the checklist could ship anything, so the marginal question is whether a *build* should refuse. It cheaply can — see the third option below.

**A second hole, found here, that no suite covers.** `KitsGen` takes a pair's `name`, `ls` (tracking) and `lh` (line height) from **`watch-fonts.json`** (lines 80, 142, 145 of the plugin), not from `kits.json`. `watch-fonts.json`'s pair block is a Python regex's copy of `theme.js`'s `PAIRS`, and no test holds it to `kits.json` or to `theme.js` — the only pair-level assertion, `type.task.weight == fonts.pairs[id].w`, reads `watch-fonts.json` on both sides. The two fixtures agree today (13 pairs × 4 fields, 0 differences — because they were regenerated in the same round). Change a pair's `ls` in `theme.js`, run `gen-kits.mjs`, forget `gen-watch-fonts.py`: both drift tests pass, and every task line on the wrist keeps last week's tracking with nothing red. That is a third copy of pair data, which is the thing the prompt forbade.

**A third option existed, and it is not node-in-the-build.** In order of how honest each is:

- *Node at its absolute path inside the prebuild command.* The plugin's command runs under SwiftPM's sandbox (writes confined to the work directory) and the path is `~/.cache/codex-runtimes/…`, which exists on this machine because of an unrelated tool's runtime and on no other. A build that depends on it fails on a clean clone with an error nobody can act on. Worse than a fixture.
- *An `xcscheme` pre-action.* Both schemes are shared, so it would be versioned, but it runs the same machine-specific node, and `swift test` does not read schemes.
- *A git pre-commit hook.* Not versioned, opt-in per clone, and there is no CI to enforce it. Weakest.
- *An mtime comparison in the plugin.* git does not preserve mtimes; a fresh clone gives arbitrary order. False positives and false negatives.
- *A source-hash stamp, checked by the plugin.* `gen-kits.mjs` writes a hash of `theme.js` into `kits.json`; `KitsGen` — a Swift process with Foundation, no node needed — hashes `theme.js` at plan time and on mismatch emits `Diagnostics.warning("kits.json was generated from a different theme.js — run node test/tools/gen-kits.mjs")`. About fifteen lines each side. The project's zero-warnings rule turns it into a blocker without making it a hard error. Its one false positive is a `theme.js` edit that does not move the table (a comment), which costs a one-second regeneration that refuses to write on a broken palette anyway. The same stamp in `watch-fonts.json` (Python can hash) closes the pair-data hole.

**What it costs now.** Two fixtures, two generators (177 lines of JS, 389 of Python), a 197-line plugin, the drift tests, and the project's only dependency outside the toolchain (`fontTools` + `brotli`, needed to regenerate and never to build). Regeneration is by hand. The `expr` carried inside the fixture — one definition of the mapping, run by the generator, the Node test and the Swift test — is the best small idea in the round.

**What it will cost later.** A nineteenth kit is really a nineteenth *and* a twentieth (partners are mutual and `Kits.day.count == 8 && Kits.night.count == 8` is asserted), plus edits to eleven pinned counts across `theme.test.js`, `gen-kits.mjs` and `KitFixtureTests.swift` (`CURATED.length, 18` three times; `fx.kits.length, 18 / openIds 16 / secretIds 2`; `want.count == 16`; `kits.count == 18`; the 8/8 pins) — deliberate pins, since the generator refuses to write on anything less, but a dozen edits. If the new kit brings a new pair: the Python run, new `.ttf`s, four `pbxproj` lines each, a `WatchInfo.plist` line each, and the three `13` pins. What it does **not** touch is `KitsGen`, `Kits.swift`, `WatchTheme.swift` or the picker — they read the table. That is the design paying for itself.

**What changing course would cost today.** The two fixes: point `KitsGen` at `kits.json`'s `pairs` for `name/ls/lh` (or add a 13 × 4 cross-assertion to `KitFixtureTests`), and add the hash stamp — about thirty lines over two generators, one plugin and one test. No user sees anything.

**Recommendation: keep the shape, change two details.** The fixture-plus-drift-test was the right choice over parsing `theme.js` (rightly ruled out by arithmetic) and over node-in-the-build (machine-specific). But the round wrote "one source of truth" and shipped three copies of the pair data with a test on two of them, and it left the build blind to drift when a fifteen-line pure-Swift check would have made it see. Both are cheap; both are the exact failure the write-up says it was guarding against.

### 4. Thirty-three font faces bundled onto the Watch

**The call.** Every one of the 13 pairs' faces — the task face at its one weight, the ui face at the two weights its axis string names — instanced from the repo's own `woff2` into static `.ttf` with `fontTools`, names read back from the produced binaries, 33 files, 1,238,128 bytes.

**Who made it.** The prompt said bundle the type (Phase 4 §3: *"bundle each kit's faces into the watch target; type is the single biggest lever against blandness"*). The number, the weights and the method were the round's.

**What each alternative costs, from `test/fixtures/watch-fonts.json`:**

| option | files | bytes | of shipped |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| shipped — task + every ui weight, all 13 pairs | 33 | 1,238,128 | 100 % |
| open kits' pairs only (drop `baloo`, `fredoka`) | 31 | 1,157,580 | 93 % |
| task + one ui weight (regular), 13 pairs | 24 | 938,264 | 76 % |
| task + one ui weight (bold), 13 pairs | 22 | 861,112 | 70 % |
| task faces only; ui in the system font | 13 | 561,248 | 45 % |
| task faces only, open kits | 11 | 480,700 | 39 % |
| system font, kit colours only | 0 | 0 | 0 % |

Two numbers that reframe it. The plan measured the 26 **un-instanced** variable TTFs at 1.54 MB, so instancing to 33 statics made the bundle *smaller* than shipping the variable files while also fixing `.weight()` being a no-op on Fraunces and Source Serif 4. And the `woff2` sources are 705 KB, but watchOS's acceptance of `woff2` is undocumented (the plan found `WOFF2Container` in `libFontParser` and rightly did not lean on it).

**What it bought.** The kit's type on the wrist — the prompt's "biggest lever" — in both processes: the complication borrows the 33 by path (two `[REDACTED]()` from the appex) rather than carrying a second copy, which the plan had budgeted at 1.5 MB and 26 hand edits and Track C proved unnecessary. The bold ui weight is not decoration: `theme.ui(…, bold: true)` draws the count, the headline, the finale card, the pills and the picker's names, and CoreText's synthetic bold for a missing weight is the thing a typographer can spot from across a room.

**What it costs now.** 1.24 MB in a watch app whose Swift is 4,914 lines — the fonts are likely more than the code. 132 hand-written `pbxproj` lines (33 × 4) and 33 `UIAppFonts` entries. A Python dependency to regenerate. Thirty-three registrations at every process start (not measured by the round). A licence audit that turned up one real defect (PT Sans carried no licence pointer) and one open legal question (15 faces across 9 families carry a Reserved Font Name through a format conversion), now written down for the owner.

**What it will cost later.** A renamed or updated font touches the `.ttf`, `watch-fonts.json`, `WatchInfo.plist`, four `pbxproj` lines and `Fonts/README.md`; the PostScript name in Swift follows automatically. A Google Fonts update to a family churns every regenerated file (fontTools rewrites `head.modified`; the round noticed and deliberately did not regenerate for the licence commit). Each new pair costs a fontTools run and the same per-file tax.

**Recommendation: keep the 33 for now; the one option worth a wrist's verdict is 24 files.** "Task + one ui weight" saves a quarter of the bytes and costs the bold in six call sites; whether a bold ui weight is distinguishable at 12–16 pt on a 41 mm screen is a question only a wrist answers, and the self-test's own "told apart by ink = 1" for the mono pair says how small that difference already is. Do not drop the two Secret-only faces: the round's argument — a kit that arrives at runtime must find its faces, and a pair is not a secret — is right, and the saving is 80 KB. The system-font option loses the round's purpose; on-demand resources do not exist on watchOS.

### 5. The Watch gets its own Day/Night picker instead of following the phone

**The call.** Two slots and a hand switch, chosen on the wrist, stored in the App Group, behind the long press on the count; no `system` mode (watchOS has no light appearance), no `schedule` (dropped by choice).

**Who made it.** Instructed. Phase 4 §3: *"Do not build the phone-follows channel. Theme is already a per-device preference in this app, and the Watch is a device. A Watch theme picker. Day and Night slots on the Watch, chosen on the Watch, stored on the Watch… behind the long press that already holds Start again, or a third page. Your call; log it."* The placement was the round's; it took the long press.

**What it bought.** A wrist that can be Paper while the phone is Ocean; no channel to build; persistence proven across a cold launch (`-TFThemeSet` calls the picker's own two store methods); the complication reads the same three keys. The round also caught, while doing it, that the old hold was gated on `canEdit` — so a person whose only list is view-only could never have changed their theme — and ungated it.

**What it costs now.** Start again lost its gesture: the hold that *was* Start again now opens a two-row sheet (Phase 5 makes it three with *Lists*), one more tap on the one action a person had learned. Changing a theme is a 0.5 s hold, a *Theme* row, a slot button and a kit row. The slot button flips the live slot as a side effect of choosing which slot to edit (item 12 below). And a cost the round did not say out loud: **the wrist has no automation at all.** The web has three switch modes; the Watch has one — by hand. A person whose phone goes to Night at 7 pm every evening flips the wrist by hand, every evening, or never. Most people will choose one kit and never flip, which makes the second slot on the wrist mostly ornamental.

**What it will cost later.** Nothing that grows. If phone-follows is ever wanted, `extra` carries a slot for free and the two designs coexist.

**The alternatives.** (a) Phone-follows, as ruled out: zero wrist UI and the phone's automation drives the wrist. Cheaper than the round's write-up allows — the payload passes unknown keys through — but with one real snag the round did not name: the phone app only reconciles on navigation and launch, so it would learn of a theme flip made in the open page late, unless the page announced it. (b) One slot on the wrist, no Day/Night: the two-slot shape without automation is the web's model carried over without the part that made it useful. (c) Follow the phone's *slot* only, kits chosen on the wrist: the phone's automation decides Day or Night, the wrist decides which kits — the best of both, for one additive field and a "Follow iPhone" toggle.

**What changing course would cost today.** (c) is roughly forty lines on the phone (send `activeSlot` with the vault, and on reconcile), twenty on the Watch, a test, and it is user-visible, so its own build.

**Recommendation: keep the picker; genuinely open on (c).** The prompt was explicit and reasoned, and the round's "the honest version is 'we will not'" was the right register. What the round should have added is the sentence above: without `system` or `schedule`, the Watch's Day slot is a thing nobody will flip. Said plainly, it turns "do not build the channel" from a rule into a trade the owner can weigh.

### 6. Reversing the 1.11 accent pin, and whether it shipped alone

**The call.** Each kit's accent is its own again (`Paper #C8321F`, `Terminal #4AF07A`; only Dark keeps `#A86014`, as its own colour); the mark stays `#A86014` everywhere.

**Who made it.** Instructed. Phase 4 §1: *"Stop pinning `--accent` to `BRAND_ACCENT`; let the kit drive it. The mark does not change."* The design is right and the round proved it the right way — `tools/kitshots.js` rendered all 18 kits before writing a test, and the picture (an amber block on a phosphor-green terminal) is the finding the contrast arithmetic could not produce. Contrast on Terminal went 4.12 → 13.29; fifteen kits' `cssText()` are byte-identical before and after (the kits agent confirmed: 3 of 18 differ, Light by one token).

**How it shipped, from the history.** `origin/main`'s first-parent line shows one merge at the end of the round. The four build stamps — 202 (8 Sep 22:42), 207 (22:55), 212 (23:26), 216 (9 Sep 00:11) — were all on the branch; only 216 reached Pages (`7341981`: "pointed at the deployed 216"; the write-up's only 212 is "a local 158 → 212 deploy"). Between 212 and 216 came the palette stamp `59ef2ee`, the clock scrim `ed89bd7` and the licence `1aada68`. What a browser fetched at 216 that it had not at 158: `theme.js` (+170 lines: the accent, `PALETTE_REV`, the Secret-word comment), `index.html` and `about.html` (the stamp, the CSP hashes, a Typefaces section), `panels.js` (the *Yours* gate), `fonts/OFL.txt`, and Light's danger token — eight files. What a TestFlight install got at 216: all of that plus the fonts, the picker, the confetti, the doorbell and the complications.

**Against the rule the same round applied to `derive()`.** `0c83a5d` pulled the `derive()` floor fix onto its own branch because it "is the one change that moves without asking… a `T2:` code on a shared list renders differently on every device the morning after the deploy, with nobody having touched anything." Its measured reach, from the same round: **0 of 2,000** *Surprise me* themes and 0 of 4 real-shaped codes moved. The accent change also moves without asking — on every device whose slot is Paper or Terminal, which since 1.11 has been every device that never chose. The round shipped the change with the wider reach inside a five-thing build and held back the one with the narrower reach to ship alone.

**Both sides, properly.** The round's rule and the owner's are not the same sentence. The round's: a change that crosses *between people* (through the shared document) ships alone. The owner's, as put to this review: a change that reaches *a person's screen without any action of theirs* ships alone. Under the round's phrasing it was consistent — the accent is "confined to a kit somebody chose by name" (though a default is not chosen). Under the owner's it was not. For shipping together: the accent change *was* the round's instructed purpose; its defence, the palette stamp, was only discovered from a field report during the round and landed between 212 and 216 — an accent-alone deploy at 202 would have gone out without `data-tokens-rev`, and the "amber persisted" report would have had nothing to settle it; and the web deploy at 216 was, in practice, the accent plus its defences plus small things. Against: attribution is the whole point of the rule, and when the field report came in the deploy carried four other web-visible changes; the Watch build number and the web deploy were the same "216" though they are different units with different audiences; and the round did four stamp cycles anyway, so one more, with Track A merged to `main` first, was cheap.

**What changing course would cost today.** Nothing to change in the code. In process: one written definition of the rule (I would take the owner's phrasing), applied to the *deploy unit* — the set of fetched web files, or an app upload — rather than to the build number that stamps both.

**Recommendation: keep the design; change the process.** The round should have said, at 0c83a5d, "by this rule the accent change goes alone too, and here is why I am not doing that" — the argument existed (the stamp was not ready), it was just not made. Write the rule down once, in `COMPATIBILITY.md` §7 where the checklist lives, and name the unit it applies to.

### 7. A hand-written `TodaysFive.xcodeproj`

**The call.** No XcodeGen, no Tuist; `project.pbxproj` is written by hand with ids of the form `TF` + nineteen zeros + three digits, banded by concern.

**Who made it.** Chosen, in Phase 2 (`8085aad`: "No XcodeGen, so the project file is written by hand"), before this range. Phases 3 and 4 added two targets and 33 font references to it by hand without revisiting.

**Measured.** 304 lines at Phase 2 → 708 after Phase 3 (+404: two targets, twenty-four files) → 853 after Phase 4 (+145: the fonts are 132 lines, 33 × 4, plus `OFL.txt`). 177 distinct hand-numbered ids, highest 469. The bands the Phase 4 plan reserved were respected: `PBXFileReference` 264–297 used of 264–299, `PBXBuildFile` 436–469 of 436+. Phase 5 took 298 and 470 for `WatchDiagnostics.swift`; **one `PBXFileReference` slot remains in the band.**

**Phase 5, measured rather than feared.** Each of the four `p5-*` branches differs from `main` in this file by the same four inserted lines (the `WatchDiagnostics` registration), at the same four places (after lines 17, 112, 235, 561), because all four branches start from the shared commit `bd45872`; the four branch copies of the file are identical to each other (0 diff lines). `git merge-tree --write-tree` on all six pairs: clean, rc 0. So "four tracks edit the same file at once" did not happen — **by discipline, not by format**: the orchestrator registered the one new file before any track started and wrote *"No track adds a Swift file; new code goes in the file that owns the concern."* That rule is the cost: the project file is shaping the code. `WatchDiagnostics.swift` is 402 lines in one file because adding a second file was off-limits; `AddFlowView.swift` grew by 589 lines in a branch for the same reason.

**What it costs now.** Four lines and a hand-picked id per file; a build-number bump is twelve lines in a file no suite checks ("the sixth home no suite checks"); the bands are a convention that has run out; and the format is `objectVersion = 56` (the Xcode 14 explicit-file format), so every file is enumerated.

**What it will cost later.** A UI test target (call 8) ≈ 100–130 lines by the complications target's footprint (its ids appear on 49 lines before its two configurations). Every new source file, forever, is an edit to a file whose ids are hand-allocated. The first time anyone opens the project in Xcode and touches a setting, Xcode rewrites the file in its own order.

**The alternatives, honestly.** *XcodeGen*: absent, `brew` absent, `mint` absent; installable without Homebrew from the release zip (a network fetch once, which the owner does routinely for TestFlight); `project.yml` for three targets is 60–90 lines; but then the `.xcodeproj` is generated and either committed (drift) or ignored (every clone regenerates), and it is a new dependency for three targets. *Tuist*: heavier, its own DSL, an install script. *A Swift package for the app targets*: SwiftPM cannot produce a watchOS app bundle or an app extension — say it plainly, it is not an option. *`xcodebuild`*: cannot generate projects. *Keep hand-written but generate the repetitive parts*: a script that emits the 33 font entries is a tool to maintain a workaround. **The one alternative the round did not consider: Xcode's own newer format.** Since Xcode 16 a project group can be a `[REDACTED]` (`objectVersion` 77): the folder on disk *is* the membership, with no per-file `PBXFileReference` or `PBXBuildFile`, exceptions expressed as a small exception set, and targets listing `[REDACTED]`. It is what File → New → Project writes today, it needs no tool, and it is still a hand-writable text file. Converting `TodaysFiveWatch/`, `[REDACTED]/`, `Shared/` and `Fonts/` would remove on the order of 250 lines, the id bands, and the "no track adds a file" rule at once (`UIAppFonts` stays 33 plist lines).

**What changing course would cost today.** To synchronized folders: rewrite the four groups and bump `objectVersion`, then prove it with both simulator builds *and* the device archive (the archive is the only compiler pass that speaks for a real watch, as `923714c` showed). No user sees anything. I have not built it; the archive must be the judge. To XcodeGen: the `yml`, the binary, and the commit-or-ignore decision.

**Recommendation: change, modestly.** Keep the project hand-written — XcodeGen is not worth a dependency for three targets — but move the four folders to synchronized groups. Phases 3 and 4 were the moment to do it (the fonts alone were 132 lines of the kind the format exists to remove) and Phase 5's "no new Swift files" rule is the bill coming due. If the archive refuses the format, fall back to keeping the bands and widening them, and say so.

### 8. Testing the Watch through launch arguments rather than driving the interface

**The call.** Seventeen `-TF…` launch arguments across the Apple targets (twelve on the Watch: `-TFWatchDemo`, `-TFWatchSelfTest`, `-TFAddSelfTest`, `-TFFontSelfTest`, `-TFConfettiSelfTest`, `-TFFaceProbe`, `-TFThemeSet`, `-TFShow`, `-TFKit`, `-TFFinale`, `-TFFinaleHold`, plus `-TFDumpWatchSend` on the phone), each performing a rule and printing a tally, because "`simctl` cannot tap a watch simulator."

**Who made it.** Chosen. Phase 3: *"Nine verification bullets had no driver, so the Watch gets a self-test"* — the same answer Phase 2 gave the phone.

**What it bought.** Checks a screenshot cannot make and a person would not: ten shuffles never repeating a line; the finale firing 0.333 s after the last check-off and not on a remote completion; 96/96 faces resolving by PostScript name, with the mono pair told apart by ink because advance width could not; 108/108 confetti assertions, which is what found `Kit.shapes` flattened to hearts-only; the picker's store surviving a cold launch; a 1,178-byte payload; the appex's arithmetic reaching 33 files. They are cheap (a `print` and `simctl launch --console-pty`), they run with no phone and no network, and they found real bugs.

**What they missed, and why it is structural.** Both of Phase 5's "looks present, does nothing" bugs are controls that were never *pressed*. `-TFAddSelfTest` calls `AddService.add` directly and never presented the dictation controller; its one interface claim, `[REDACTED]=present`, meant "a branch would be taken", and the Phase 5 prompt is right that it was read as more. `-TFThemeSet` calls the picker's store methods, not its rows. Nothing in three rounds tapped the title button. A launch argument can prove a rule; it cannot prove that a finger reaches it. The round's write-ups say "the picker was never touched by a finger" and treat that as a fact of the machine. It was a fact of the chosen instrument.

**What the machine actually has.** Measured on this Mac (`review/raw/calls/xcui-watch-platform.txt`): `WatchSimulator.platform/Developer/Library/Frameworks/` contains `XCTest.framework`, **`XCUIAutomation.framework`**, `Testing.framework`, and `Xcode/Agents/XCTRunner.app`. `XCUIElement.tap`, `doubleTap`, `press(forDuration:)`, `swipeUp/Down/Left/Right` carry only `API_UNAVAILABLE(tvos)`. **`XCUIDevice.perform(handGesture: .doubleTap)` is `API_AVAILABLE(watchos(10.0))`** — so Double Tap *can* be injected on a simulator, which contradicts Phase 3 ("no way to inject the gesture on a simulator, so it is written and left unverified") and Phase 5's plan ("There is no way to inject the gesture"). `[REDACTED]` exists. `XCUISiriService.activate(voiceRecognitionText:)` is in the watch platform's headers with no watchOS exclusion (two symbols in the binary) — whether it works on a watch simulator is untested here and I do not claim it. `simctl` has no tap subcommand on *any* platform; on the iPhone the drivers people use are XCUITest and the Simulator app, and on the watch simulator XCUITest is the sanctioned one. "`simctl` cannot tap a watch" was true and beside the point.

**Would it have caught the caret?** A test: `app.navigationBars.buttons.firstMatch.tap()` then assert the picker's *Lists* title appears. Hit-testing a `Button` inside the `navigationTitle { }` slot is the same SwiftUI/UIKit code on simulator and device — it is not a hardware fact — so if the title does not receive the tap on the wrist it very probably does not on the simulator either, and the test fails. Phase 5 still does not know which of "tap never arrives" and "tap arrives, presentation fails" it is (`64ca8a1` removed the caret and instrumented the press); either way a UI test would have measured the in-app path, which no instrument did. Probably caught.

**Would it have caught the +?** Phase 3 measured "lands on the QWERTY keyboard" from a throwaway probe, not from the app's `+` inside its `.sheet`. Phase 5's diagnosis (`3e17582`) is a WatchKit modal raised from underneath a SwiftUI sheet, "the textbook swallowed presentation". If the sheet swallows it on the simulator too — a view-hierarchy fact, not a microphone fact — a test that taps `+` → *Add* and asserts that any new screen appears fails on the simulator, and the bug is caught a round early. If the simulator shows the chooser from inside the sheet and the device does not, the test passes while the wrist fails. Nobody measured the in-app path, so the honest answer is: unknown, and a UI test would have converted "assumed" into "measured" either way. What no simulator test can see: the microphone, the wrist's real hit targets, Always-On, and a completion that never returns after a screen did appear.

**The cost.** A UI test target in the hand-written `pbxproj` ≈ 100–130 lines plus a `Testables` entry in `TodaysFiveWatch.xcscheme` (which today is empty). `xcodebuild test -scheme TodaysFiveWatch -destination 'platform=watchOS Simulator,id=…'` on the booted pair; the app runs independently (`[REDACTED]`), `-TFWatchDemo` seeds a list with no phone, and the vault works because UI tests run the signed app. Each case 10–20 s on a watch simulator; a suite of eight is two or three minutes, locally — the same "no CI" the self-tests already live with. The round's stated reason for not doing it was a limitation of `simctl`, and `simctl` was never the tool.

**Recommendation: change — add both, not either.** Keep the self-tests for rules. Add a small XCUITest target for the Watch — the title/list switch, `+` presents an input screen, `perform(handGesture: .doubleTap)` presents it, the long press opens the sheet, a picker row changes the stored kit, a view-only list refuses, the Add complication's URL opens Today — written against Phase 5's controls, since those are what ship next. And correct the two write-ups' claim that Double Tap cannot be injected.

## The pattern

Across the three rounds the calls that were **measured** were almost always right, and the calls that were **reasoned** were right in proportion to how close the reasoning stayed to something the round could touch. The accent revert was settled by a picture; the fixture-not-parser call by counting computed tokens; the doorbell by a virtual clock and then a live socket; the scrim by pixel values; the 32-bit `Int` by an archive. When the round chose against an instruction it could touch — 1.13, the picker's live slot — it wrote the argument down and the owner could reverse it. The write-ups' habit of saying what an instrument cannot see is the best thing in the range, and it is why this review could be done from a chair.

Where the rounds went wrong they went wrong the same way twice: **they mistook the instrument they had for the instruments that existed.** "`simctl` cannot tap a watch" was true and led to seventeen launch arguments, when `XCUIAutomation.framework` was sitting in the watch platform with `tap`, `press`, and `perform(handGesture: .doubleTap)` available on watchOS 10 — and the two Phase 5 bugs are exactly the class (a control never pressed) that the chosen instrument cannot see and the other one can. The same shape produced Phase 3's dictation design: an advantage nobody could observe, preferred over the API that could not fail invisibly. The rule worth taking from it is the Phase 5 prompt's, sharpened: name the instrument, and before choosing it, name the one you are not using.

The second pattern is **slogans outliving their invariants.** "Only the links" was bent within a round and the design survived, because the real rule was narrower; "one source of truth" shipped with a third copy of the pair data that no test holds; "the complication needs the decrypted document in the group" stayed in three documents after the snapshot design made it false. The rounds are excellent at recording *why* a call was made and less careful about re-reading the sentence once the next call has changed what it rests on — the round itself noticed this for `#A86014` ("a premise written down is a premise something else can invalidate") and then did it three more times.

The third is **applying a rule to the case it was written for and not to its neighbour.** The ships-alone rule was applied with real discipline to `derive()` and not to the accent change that reached more screens; the web picker's "looking must not move the slot" was argued carefully and the Watch picker did the opposite days later; the App Group was opened for a reader and kept after the reader left. None of these was a wrong call on its own terms. Each is a right call that stopped one step short of the sentence "and by this same rule, X".

What the rounds did that a reviewer should say plainly: they never hand-maintained the palette, they never put a secret in a log, they refused to fix a generator's loss in a renderer, they pulled the one change that crossed between people, and they wrote down, before each round, what only a wrist could answer — and then, when the wrist answered, the Phase 5 prompt could hold them to it. That is the process working. The recommendations above are small because the calls were mostly right; the two that matter most — a UI test target for the Watch, and the documents out of the App Group — are each an afternoon.
